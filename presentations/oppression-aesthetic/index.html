About "Your Oppression: Our Aesthetic"

This project challenges the flattening of human atrocities as represented in album art that uses such imagery for aesthetic purposes. Inspired by Bill Drummond's Silent Protest work and grounded in design justice principles, it examines how the music industry has appropriated imagery of suffering.

The presentation uses restraint and respect in its approach, providing context and critical analysis rather than reproducing exploitation.

Research Overview

This analysis examines documentary atrocity photography reappropriated as album art in anarcho-punk, hardcore, and powerviolence (ca. 1988–2017).

Key questions:

  • How does proximity to violence affect ethical evaluation?
  • What's the difference between documentation and extraction?

Context

Album artwork often serves as a visual representation of music's themes and emotions.

However, when art appropriates imagery of real human suffering, ethical questions arise:

  • Who benefits from these representations?
  • Are the subjects of these images given agency?
  • How does aestheticization affect our understanding of real atrocities?
This slide establishes the critical framework for examining album art.

Design Justice Framework

"Design is the process by which the politics of one world become the constraints on another." —Fred Turner

Following Sasha Costanza-Chock, this project asks:

  • Who benefits from these design choices?
  • Whose values are embedded in the interface?
  • What ontologies (ways of being/knowing) do we reproduce?
  • How do we move beyond inclusion toward justice, autonomy, sovereignty?
Design Justice challenges us to examine power structures embedded in design decisions.

Platform Contradictions

This project embodies its own contradictions:

  • Using corporate platforms (GitHub, Jekyll) to critique corporate appropriation
  • Creating an academic artifact about DIY punk culture
  • Reproducing the "male gaze" of collecting/cataloguing violence
  • Speaking about rather than with affected communities

These contradictions are made visible rather than hidden—part of honest methodology.

Transparency about contradictions is itself a Design Justice principle.

Questions of Proximity

Each album artwork raises questions about distance from lived experience:

  • Are bands close to the violence they portray?
  • What's the difference between documentation and appropriation?
  • How does context collapse in digital circulation?
  • Who has the right to represent atrocity?

These questions have no simple answers—they require case-by-case analysis.

Proximity doesn't determine legitimacy, but it matters for ethical analysis.

Pre-Internet Context

These albums (primarily early 1990s) circulated in underground networks where documentary atrocity photography functioned as counter-information.

Key tensions:

  • Images did circulate information not available in mainstream media
  • But affordance was shock and political identification, not humanization
  • Photography's human subjects were "disafforded" by design decisions
  • Context collapse: specific atrocities became generic signifiers
Acknowledges historical function as counter-information while maintaining critical analysis.
