# Code-Switching and Identity
_This lecture was not completed in class because we switched to an ePortfolio workshop._

## Today's Readings

- **Suarez**: Code-switching is strategic and legitimate

- **Santos Neto**: But it's also painful—you lose something in translation

*Both examine living between codes*

## Suarez: Spanglish is Real

- Not "broken English" or "bad Spanish"—it's a legitimate linguistic practice with rules

- Code-switching is strategic:
	- "Parquin" for parking (shorter than "estacionamiento")
	- "Linkear" for click (adapted for computers)

*This isn't laziness—it's efficiency and adaptation*

## Suarez: Critical Language Awareness

- Language mixing is purposeful

- Speakers make conscious choices about when to switch

- Different contexts require different codes

- There's no "pure" language—all languages borrow and mix

## Santos Neto's "Tug of War"

- Writing in Portuguese vs. English:
	- Different languages carry different identities
	- Translation loses cultural meaning
	- Sometimes you can't fully express yourself in your "second" language

*Even when that language is the one you're required to write in*

## Santos Neto's Process

His specific struggle:

- Thinks in Portuguese when brainstorming and analyzing

- Translates to English when writing—slows down his process

- Academic contexts require English, but Portuguese carries his full identity

## The Tension

- **Suarez**: Code-switching is strategic and rule-governed

- **Santos Neto**: But it's also painful—you lose something in translation

- Both show living between languages/codes is complex:
	- It's a skill
	- But it's also a burden

## Activity 2: Language and Identity

She claims you can't separate who you are from how you speak.

Let's test this with your own experience.

**Format**: Individual → Small Group → Whole Class

**Time**: 20 minutes total

## Individual Reflection

**Option 1**: A time when someone criticized your language and it felt personal
- What did they criticize? (grammar, accent, word choice, slang)
- Who made the criticism?
- How did it feel? Why did it feel like more than just a "correction"?

**Option 2**: A time when you had to suppress your natural way of speaking
- What context required you to change?
- What specifically did you have to hide or change?
- How did it feel to "perform" a different version of yourself?

## Small Group Discussion

Share your reflections:

- What's the connection between how you speak and who you are?

- When someone corrects your language, what are they really correcting?

- Can you separate your identity from your language? Should you have to?

## Whole Class Discussion

Each group shares one insight:

- How is language connected to identity?

- What's at stake when you're forced to code-switch?
