# T&T Glossary
## Glossary of Key Concepts

### Ad-Creep

A term describing the expansion of advertising into spaces, media, and aspects of daily life where it was previously absent. Ad-creep marks the encroachment of commercial messaging into schools, public spaces, digital platforms, and even personal interactions, often blurring the boundaries between content and advertisement. In digital contexts, it refers to practices like targeted ads, sponsored content, and product placement embedded in social media or apps. For textual and technological studies, ad-creep illustrates how economic imperatives shape media environments, attention economies, and cultural production.

### Advertising in Games

In Persuasive Games (2007), Ian Bogost outlines how advertising functions within digital games, distinguishing between structural forms and rhetorical strategies:

- **Advergames** — Entire games created for promotional purposes, with play designed around a brand, product, or service.
- **In-Game Advertising** — Branded content inserted into existing games, such as billboards, logos, or virtual product displays.
- **Product Placement** — Branded items integrated into gameplay mechanics or narrative (e.g., driving a branded car), making the product part of the player’s activity.
- **Product Tie-Ins** — Games released in connection with other media franchises, functioning as marketing extensions of those properties.

Bogost further identifies rhetorical categories that describe how games persuade through advertising:
- **Associative** — Linking brands with desirable contexts, values, or lifestyles within the game environment.
- **Illustrative** — Using gameplay to depict how a product works or fits into everyday life.
- **Demonstrative** — Allowing players to perform actions with or through the product, enacting its use or value procedurally.

Taken together, these forms show how games do more than host advertising; they simulate persuasive logics, making consumption and branding part of the procedural rhetoric of play.

### Algorithms vs. Analytics

A conceptual distinction that highlights two complementary but distinct modes of computational reasoning in digital culture and scholarship.

- **Algorithms** refer to the rules and procedures — often automated — that structure how data are processed, sorted, and acted upon. They embody decision-making logics, determining what content is shown, prioritized, or hidden across digital systems. In this sense, algorithms are performative: they do things in the world.
- **Analytics**, by contrast, refers to the interpretive examination of data and its patterns. Analytics seeks to understand what data reveal about systems, users, or cultures, emphasizing reflection and insight rather than automation.

In digital humanities and media studies, distinguishing algorithms from analytics clarifies the relationship between computation and interpretation: algorithms make operations possible, while analytics makes those operations legible. Scholars often critique how algorithmic systems shape social life invisibly, while exploring how analytical methods can reintroduce accountability, transparency, and human judgment into data-driven research.

### ANT (Actor-Network Theory)

A theoretical framework developed by Bruno Latour, Michel Callon, and John Law, which explores the interconnected relationships between human and non-human actors (including objects, technologies, and concepts) within networks. ANT challenges traditional distinctions between society, nature, and technology by treating all elements as equally important "actors" that shape and are shaped by their interactions. It emphasizes the ways in which power, agency, and meaning are distributed across networks, rather than being located solely in human subjects. ANT is used to analyze how networks stabilize or change over time and is particularly valuable in the study of science, technology, and society.

### Bender Rule, the

A principle formulated by computational linguist Emily M. Bender that states:

    Always name the language you’re working with.

The rule calls attention to the Anglocentric bias in computational and digital research, where English is too often treated as the default or universal language. By explicitly identifying the language of a dataset, corpus, or model, researchers make visible the linguistic assumptions shaping their work and acknowledge the diversity of linguistic systems that exist beyond English. In technology and literacy studies, the Bender Rule underscores the importance of recognizing how digital tools and infrastructures privilege certain languages — and, by extension, certain cultures and epistemologies — over others. Applying the rule challenges the notion of technological neutrality and encourages more inclusive, multilingual approaches to digital literacy, data, and interface design. In “Language Is Not a Default Setting: Countering DH’s English Problem” (2023), Quinn Dombrowski and Patrick J. Burns invoke the Bender Rule to critique the dominance of English in the Digital Humanities. They argue that DH tools, training materials, and publication venues overwhelmingly assume English-language users and data, marginalizing other linguistic communities. Following the Bender Rule means designing projects that explicitly state their linguistic scope, support under-resourced languages, and cultivate a truly global DH.

### Big Tent Digital Humanities

A metaphor used within the digital humanities to describe the field’s openness to a wide range of methods, disciplines, and scholarly practices. Emerging in the 2000s as DH began consolidating its identity, the “big tent” frames DH as inclusive, interdisciplinary, and pluralistic — encompassing everything from textual encoding and data mining to cultural criticism, pedagogy, and creative practice. While celebrated for its capaciousness, the metaphor has also been critiqued for masking exclusions, power dynamics, and institutional boundaries that determine who and what gets to belong under the tent.

### Bramble (in Hypertext Fiction)

Refers to a complex, branching narrative structure in hypertext fiction that resembles the tangled, interconnected pathways of a bramble bush. In this context, a "bramble" describes a hypertext narrative with many possible routes or nodes, where readers may encounter dead ends, loops, or multiple overlapping paths. The term emphasizes the challenge of navigating such a narrative, where choices lead to unexpected or nonlinear outcomes, and the reader's experience may vary dramatically depending on the route taken. This structure contrasts with more straightforward or linear forms of storytelling, offering a rich, exploratory engagement with the text.

### Boundary Work

A concept developed by sociologist Thomas Gieryn to describe the practices of establishing, reinforcing, or negotiating the boundaries between different domains, such as science and non-science, academic disciplines, or professional fields. Boundary work involves the processes through which people or institutions create distinctions to legitimize their authority, expertise, or knowledge. These boundaries can define what is considered credible or legitimate within a field and can include efforts to demarcate professional jurisdictions or maintain institutional autonomy.

Building on Gieryn's work, Julie Thompson Klein extends the concept to include the dynamics of interdisciplinary and transdisciplinary research. Klein explores how boundary work not only delineates disciplines but also facilitates the crossing and reconfiguration of these boundaries. She emphasizes that boundary work involves not just separation but also collaboration and integration, where disciplines interact to solve complex problems. Klein highlights the importance of "boundary objects"—concepts, methods, or tools that operate across multiple disciplines—to bridge gaps and foster shared understanding. Her work underscores the active role of boundary work in both maintaining and challenging disciplinary structures, enabling innovation and cross-disciplinary knowledge production.
BNP (British National Party)

A far-right, nationalist political party in the UK. Ahmed critiques the BNP as an example of how whiteness and exclusionary practices are normalized within certain political and cultural contexts. By referencing the BNP, Ahmed highlights the ways in which institutional racism is both perpetuated and resisted, drawing attention to how such ideologies contrast sharply with institutional diversity rhetoric.
Cartesian Mind

Refers to the concept of mind as formulated by the philosopher René Descartes, who proposed a dualistic distinction between mind and body. In Cartesian dualism, the mind is characterized as a non-material, thinking entity (res cogitans) distinct from the physical body (res extensa). The mind, for Descartes, is the seat of consciousness, reasoning, and self-awareness, and operates independently of the body, which is governed by mechanical laws. This concept has influenced various fields, including philosophy, cognitive science, and discussions of human identity and agency.
Collaborators' Bill of Rights

A set of principles created to guide fair and ethical collaboration in digital humanities and other interdisciplinary projects. Originally developed during the “Off the Tracks” workshop at the University of Maryland in 2011, the *Collaborators' Bill of Rights* emphasizes recognizing contributions, valuing diverse skills, and ensuring transparency and shared decision-making among project members. It advocates for crediting all contributors equitably, regardless of their roles or institutional status, and promotes the establishment of clear agreements on intellectual property, authorship, and responsibilities. The document highlights the importance of respect, communication, and inclusivity in collaborative projects, particularly when blending technical, scholarly, and creative domains.
Communities of Practice

A concept developed by Jean Lave and Étienne Wenger to describe groups of individuals who come together to share and build knowledge around a common interest or practice. These communities are characterized by three key elements: a shared domain of interest, a community of members who engage and interact regularly, and a shared practice involving a repertoire of experiences, tools, stories, and methods. Through social interaction and collaborative learning, members of a community of practice develop their skills, refine their practices, and contribute to a collective knowledge base. This concept highlights the role of social learning in professional and academic contexts, emphasizing that expertise is built through participation and interaction within a community.
Constructive Hypertext

A type of hypertext that allows users to not only navigate through pre-existing links and content but also to actively contribute, modify, or extend the hypertext structure. Unlike more static forms of hypertext, where the pathways between nodes are fixed by the author, constructive hypertext encourages participatory engagement by enabling readers to add their own connections, annotations, or new content.
Cultural Analytics

A term coined by Lev Manovich to describe the use of computational and data-visualization methods to analyze large-scale cultural datasets — such as images, films, social media posts, or texts — in order to reveal patterns, trends, and aesthetic forms that are difficult to perceive through traditional close reading. Cultural analytics combines quantitative and qualitative approaches, using algorithms to explore visual or textual corpora while maintaining attention to context, interpretation, and meaning. Manovich frames it as a new paradigm for the digital humanities: a way to study not just a few canonical works, but the “long tail” of cultural production, allowing researchers to map both mainstream and peripheral cultural expressions within massive digital archives.
Cultural Studies

An interdisciplinary field of study that examines the ways in which culture—understood as a set of practices, values, beliefs, and forms of expression—shapes and is shaped by society, politics, and power structures. Emerging in the mid-20th century, particularly through the work of scholars at the Birmingham Centre for Contemporary Cultural Studies, cultural studies investigates the relationship between culture and identity, ideology, media, and social institutions. It draws on various disciplines, including sociology, literary theory, anthropology, and media studies, to explore issues of race, gender, class, and globalization, often with a focus on popular culture and marginalized voices.
Deconstructionism

A critical approach developed by philosopher Jacques Derrida that seeks to expose and challenge the underlying assumptions, binary oppositions, and contradictions within texts. Deconstructionism questions the stability of meaning, arguing that language is inherently unstable and open to multiple interpretations. By deconstructing texts, it reveals how concepts such as truth, identity, and meaning are constructed through language and how they can unravel when examined closely. This approach is often associated with poststructuralism and has been influential in fields such as literary theory, philosophy, and cultural studies.
Deepfake

A portmanteau of “deep learning” and “fake,” deepfakes are synthetic media—most often video or audio—in which a person’s likeness is algorithmically manipulated or generated to create the illusion of speech or action that never occurred. Powered by generative adversarial networks (GANs) and other machine learning methods, deepfakes raise questions of authenticity, consent, and trust in digital media. In technology and textual studies, they highlight how identity and representation can be fabricated, remixed, or weaponized, blurring the boundaries between truth and fiction in ways that challenge cultural, ethical, and legal frameworks.
Deep Remixability

A concept developed by Lev Manovich to describe the way digital culture allows for the blending and reconfiguration of media elements at a fundamental level. Unlike traditional remixing, which involves combining existing works, deep remixability enables creators to manipulate the underlying structures, algorithms, and forms of media. This approach highlights the fluidity and versatility of digital media, where elements from different forms (e.g., text, image, video, 3D models) can be combined seamlessly, producing hybrid and innovative outcomes. Manovich's idea emphasizes the transformative potential of digital tools in shaping new forms of artistic and cultural expression.
Disorder (Condé)

In her works, Maryse Condé often explores the concept of disorder as a force that disrupts established narratives and structures, particularly in the context of colonialism, identity, and power. Disorder, for Condé, reflects the complexities and contradictions of human experience, challenging simplistic or binary frameworks. It embodies the chaos that results from the intersection of cultural, political, and historical forces, and often serves as a space where marginalized voices and alternative histories can emerge. Disorder is not merely negative but can also offer a potential for renewal and transformation.
Diversity Work

Referring both to Ahmed’s personal experience and broader theoretical explorations, this term involves the labor of promoting diversity within institutions, often done by marginalized individuals. It addresses the complexities, challenges, and resistance encountered in making institutions more inclusive.
Enframing

A concept with philosophical roots in Martin Heidegger’s essay “The Question Concerning Technology” (1954), where it names the technological ordering of the world — the way technology frames beings and nature as "standing reserves," or resources available for use, fundamentally shaping our existence and perception of use. For example, in “Black is Not the Absence of Light” (Debates in the Digital Humanities, 2023), Frazier, Hyman, and Green adapt this idea to articulate how Black digital humanities envisions the digital as accountable to Black publics. Here, enframing is both depiction and practice: digital technologies frame subjectivity and identity, while Black digital humanities insists that these frames must be oriented toward service, representation, and care for Black communities.
Ergodic Text

A type of literature that requires nontrivial effort from the reader to navigate and comprehend, often involving unconventional narrative structures, nonlinear paths, or interactive elements. The term was popularized by Espen J. Aarseth in Cybertext: Perspectives on Ergodic Literature (1997), where he contrasted ergodic texts with more traditional forms of literature that follow a more straightforward, linear reading process.
Ekphrasis

Ekphrasis is a literary device in which a work of art, usually visual, is described in a vivid and detailed manner. The purpose is to convey the experience of the artwork to the reader, often emphasizing its emotional or symbolic significance.
Enthymeme

A rhetorical device in which one of the premises in an argument is left unstated, with the expectation that the audience will infer it. Unlike a formal syllogism, where all premises are explicitly presented, an enthymeme requires the audience to fill in a missing piece of reasoning. This incomplete argument can be persuasive because it engages the audience’s participation.

Example:  
Stated: "He must be a good leader because his team is so successful."  
Unstated Premise: "Successful teams have good leaders."

In this enthymeme, the missing premise ("Successful teams have good leaders") is assumed, but not explicitly stated, allowing the audience to complete the reasoning themselves.
Exploratory Hypertext

A form of hypertext where users navigate through a fixed set of links and nodes created by the author, exploring the text by following different paths and making choices that shape their reading experience. Unlike constructive hypertext, exploratory hypertext does not allow the reader to modify or add to the text, but it still offers multiple pathways and non-linear structures that encourage discovery and interaction.
Feminist Data Manifest-NO

A manifestoLinks to an external site. discussed in Debates in the Digital Humanities (2023) that critiques dominant paradigms of data science and digital humanities. The Manifest-NO refuses extractive, universalizing, and technocratic approaches to data, instead articulating feminist commitments to refusal, care, situated knowledge, and justice. By saying “no” to harmful data practices, the authors reimagine how data can be engaged ethically and politically, centering marginalized voices and resisting reductive quantification.
Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs)

A class of machine learning models in which two neural networks — a generator and a discriminator — are trained in competition with each other. The generator creates synthetic data (such as images, audio, or text), while the discriminator evaluates whether the outputs are real (from the training data) or fake (from the generator). Through this adversarial process, GANs can produce highly realistic outputs that are often indistinguishable from real data. In cultural and textual studies, GANs are significant for their role in creating deepfakes, as well as for how they challenge notions of originality, authenticity, and creative authorship in digital media.
Geofencing

A location-based digital technology that uses GPS, RFID, Wi-Fi, or cellular data to create a virtual boundary around a physical area. When a device crosses this boundary, it can trigger automated actions such as sending notifications, tracking movement, or restricting/allowing access. In cultural and textual studies, geofencing highlights how space and mobility are monitored, managed, and commodified through digital infrastructures. It raises questions about privacy, surveillance, labor, and the politics of space, as physical presence becomes datafied and subject to algorithmic control.
Gesamtkunstwerk

A German term meaning "total work of art," referring to a synthesis of various art forms into a cohesive, immersive experience. Originally popularized by composer Richard Wagner, the concept suggests the unification of music, drama, visual arts, and architecture into a singular artistic expression that overwhelms the senses. In digital media, hypertext, and multimedia contexts, gesamtkunstwerk can describe projects that blend text, sound, video, and interactivity, creating a multifaceted and fully immersive experience for the audience. The term highlights the integration of different artistic disciplines to achieve a more profound, unified impact.
Going Meta

A phrase used to describe the act of stepping outside a system, narrative, or interaction in order to reflect on or comment about it. To “go meta” is to move to a higher-order frame: for example, a text drawing attention to its own construction, a player commenting on game mechanics while playing, or a discussion about the rules of discourse itself. In media and cultural studies, going meta highlights reflexivity, self-reference, and critique, often exposing the assumptions or boundaries of the system under consideration. In digital culture, the phrase also circulates informally (e.g., “meta memes” or “meta commentary”) to signal content that comments on its own medium or genre.
Gray Literature

Refers to research materials and publications that are not formally published through traditional commercial or academic publishing channels. These documents often include reports, white papers, theses, conference proceedings, technical documentation, government publications, and other materials produced by organizations, think tanks, or individuals. Gray literature can provide valuable insights and data that are not available in peer-reviewed journals, though its quality and reliability may vary, making careful evaluation essential when using it for research.
Habitus

Bordieu's notion of Habitus refers to the deeply ingrained habits, skills, and dispositions that individuals acquire through their life experiences. It reflects the social structures within which an individual is embedded, shaping their perceptions, thoughts, and actions.
Hyperbaton

A rhetorical device involving the deliberate rearrangement of the usual word order in a sentence to create emphasis, achieve a specific effect, or conform to a particular metrical pattern. By altering conventional syntax, hyperbaton draws attention to specific words or phrases and enhances the expressive quality of the language. Examples include phrases like "This I must see" instead of "I must see this," or "Grave danger you are in. Impatient you are" from Yoda's speech in Star Wars, where the atypical word order emphasizes certain concepts.
Identity Tourism

A term developed by Lisa Nakamura in Cybertypes: Race, Ethnicity, and Identity on the Internet (2002) to describe the practice of adopting or “trying on” racial, gendered, or cultural identities in online environments, often by those who do not belong to the group they are portraying. In early internet spaces like chatrooms, MUDs, or forums, users could experiment with alternate identities, but Nakamura critiques how such performances often reproduced stereotypes, trivialized lived experiences, and reinforced power imbalances. Identity tourism reveals how digital spaces can enable both imaginative play and problematic appropriation, raising questions about authenticity, embodiment, and the politics of representation in virtual worlds.
Interdisciplinarity

An approach to research, education, or problem-solving that integrates concepts, theories, methods, and tools from multiple disciplines to create new insights or address complex issues. Julie Thompson Klein emphasizes that interdisciplinarity goes beyond simply combining knowledge; it involves the synthesis of perspectives and the development of new frameworks, leading to the emergence of hybrid fields or solutions. This collaborative process breaks down traditional disciplinary boundaries and encourages innovative thinking by fostering connections between disciplines in ways that challenge conventional boundaries and norms.
Interface

A point of interaction between different systems, entities, or components, which facilitates communication, exchange, or control. In technology, an interface can refer to hardware (such as a keyboard or touch screen) or software (such as a graphical user interface or API) that allows users to interact with computers, devices, or digital environments. More broadly, interfaces mediate interactions between humans and machines, texts and readers, or various media forms. Scholars in media studies and digital humanities often explore how interfaces shape user experiences, influence accessibility, and mediate the flow of information, thereby impacting the ways in which knowledge is produced and consumed.
Intertextuality

A concept introduced by literary theorist Julia Kristeva, referring to the relationships and connections between texts. It suggests that no text exists in isolation but is shaped by and references other texts, whether through direct quotation, allusion, parody, or thematic resonance. Intertextuality highlights how meaning is constructed through the interaction of texts and how readers bring prior knowledge of other works to their interpretation of a text. This concept is central to postmodernism, where texts often engage in self-referential and intertextual play.
Long Tail

A concept drawn from Chris Anderson’s The Long Tail: Why the Future of Business Is Selling Less of More (2006), which Lev Manovich adapts in his discussions of digital culture and cultural analytics. The Long Tail describes how digital distribution transforms cultural economies: instead of relying on a few mass-market hits (the “head”), digital platforms sustain vast numbers of niche products, creators, and audiences (the “tail”). Manovich extends this idea beyond economics to cultural analysis, showing how the abundance of digital artifacts enables new modes of studying culture at scale — not just analyzing the canon or the popular, but the countless small, everyday, or marginal works that populate the long tail of online culture.
Logocentrism

Logocentrism is a term coined by Jacques Derrida in his critique of Western philosophy, where he argues that there is an overemphasis on speech, logic, and the written word as central forms of communication. This privileging of logos (meaning "word" or "reason") tends to marginalize other forms of expression and knowledge.
Mangles

A concept discussed by Todd Presner, building upon the work of Andrew Pickering, to describe the dynamic and iterative processes through which technology, knowledge, and social practices co-evolve. The term refers to the entangled and often unpredictable ways in which human intentions and material agencies interact, leading to outcomes that are not fully controlled by either. This concept challenges linear and deterministic narratives of technological development by highlighting the contingent and negotiated nature of change, where both human actors and non-human elements (such as technologies or natural forces) adapt and influence one another.
Marronage

Marronage refers to the act of enslaved people escaping from plantations or colonial authorities, seeking freedom and establishing autonomous communities, often in remote or difficult-to-access areas like mountains or forests. Historically linked to the Caribbean and the Americas, marronage symbolizes resistance against slavery and colonial oppression. It can be categorized into two types: petit marronage, which involves temporary escape, and grand marronage, referring to permanent flight and the formation of independent communities. The concept also extends metaphorically to represent acts of defiance, cultural resistance, and the pursuit of autonomy in oppressive systems.
Media Analytics

An interdisciplinary approach to studying media through the collection, measurement, and interpretation of data about how media are produced, circulated, and consumed. Media analytics draws from communication studies, digital humanities, and data science to examine engagement metrics, network patterns, audience behavior, and content flows across platforms. While often associated with commercial or marketing uses (e.g., social media metrics, click-through rates), scholars also adapt media analytics for critical and cultural inquiry — using data to trace patterns of representation, attention, and power in digital ecosystems. In contrast to cultural analytics (which focuses on analyzing media artifacts themselves), media analytics centers on the movements and interactions surrounding media, offering insight into the dynamics of visibility, virality, and influence in networked culture.
Media Effects

A research tradition in communication studies that examines how exposure to media influences attitudes, behaviors, emotions, or beliefs. Rooted in 20th-century mass communication research, media effects scholarship investigates phenomena such as persuasion, aggression, fear, political attitudes, and learning, often through experimental and quantitative methods. While early models (like the “hypodermic needle” theory) imagined audiences as passive recipients of powerful media messages, later work emphasizes more complex, limited, and variable effects. In the context of digital humanities and cultural studies, media effects highlights ongoing debates about the extent to which media shape individuals and societies versus how audiences actively interpret and resist media messages.
Metalanguage

A language or set of terms used to describe, analyze, or discuss another language or system of signs. In linguistics, metalanguage refers to the vocabulary and structures used to explain grammatical, semantic, or syntactical elements of a language. More broadly, it can also apply to the specialized terminology and frameworks used in fields such as programming, semiotics, or literary criticism to interpret and understand texts, codes, or symbols. Metalanguage enables the articulation and examination of complex ideas by providing a structured way to discuss how language and meaning operate.
Metamedium

A metamedium refers to a medium that can represent not only its own content but also the properties and functions of other media. The concept was popularized by Alan Kay in the context of computing, where digital platforms such as computers are described as metamedia because they can simulate a wide range of traditional and new media forms (e.g., text, images, audio, video) and can be programmed to create entirely new types of media. A metamedium thus emphasizes the flexibility, adaptability, and transformative potential of digital technologies.
Multidisciplinarity

A form of inquiry in which multiple disciplines come together to address a common issue, problem, or theme, but each discipline maintains its distinct methods and perspectives. According to Julie Thompson Klein, multidisciplinarity involves juxtaposition rather than integration. In a multidisciplinary approach, disciplines work alongside each other, contributing their unique insights without necessarily blending or synthesizing their knowledge. The outcome remains a collection of disciplinary perspectives rather than a new, unified framework.
Multilinearity

Refers to the structure of texts or narratives that do not follow a single, linear path but instead allow for multiple possible sequences or connections between elements. This concept is often applied to hypertext, digital literature, and interactive media, where readers or users can navigate through various nodes, creating a personalized, non-sequential experience. Multilinearity contrasts with traditional, linear texts, offering diverse pathways and encouraging an active role in shaping the meaning or progression of a work. It is a hallmark of ergodic literature and postmodern narratives.
Mutual Knowledge Problem

A concept in communication theory and social interactions that refers to the difficulty in establishing shared understanding between individuals or groups. The problem arises because each party must not only grasp information or a message but must also recognize that others understand it as well. This recursive nature of mutual knowledge is crucial in contexts where collaboration, negotiation, or teaching is involved, as effective communication depends on both parties confirming their mutual comprehension. The problem highlights the complexities of aligning perspectives, especially in diverse or multidisciplinary settings, and underscores the role of clear communication strategies in overcoming misunderstandings.
Non-Performativity

A concept developed by Sara Ahmed to describe speech acts, policies, or institutional gestures that claim to promote change, diversity, or inclusion but do not lead to meaningful action or transformation. In contrast to J.L. Austin's concept of performativity—where saying something is also a form of doing—Ahmed’s idea of non-performativity emphasizes situations where statements (e.g., commitments to diversity) serve more as symbolic gestures than as vehicles for actual change. Non-performativity calls attention to the gap between institutional rhetoric and lived realities, highlighting how language can be used to maintain the status quo under the guise of progressive values.
Paideia

Paideia refers to the process of education in ancient Greek culture, encompassing the upbringing and cultural education of the ideal citizen. It is associated with the development of intellectual, moral, and aesthetic faculties through a rigorous program of learning.
Paratexts

Paratexts refer to the additional materials accompanying a primary text, such as prefaces, introductions, footnotes, and other editorial elements. These materials provide context, guide interpretation, and influence how a text is received by its audience.
Phenomenology

A philosophical approach developed by Edmund Husserl and further expanded by thinkers like Martin Heidegger and Maurice Merleau-Ponty, focusing on the direct study of lived experience. Phenomenology seeks to describe phenomena as they are experienced, without relying on preconceived theories or assumptions. It emphasizes the subjective, first-person perspective, highlighting how individuals perceive, interpret, and make meaning of the world around them. In phenomenological research, the goal is to understand how people experience things, ranging from everyday objects to complex social interactions, through consciousness and intentionality, often questioning the relationship between self, body, and world.
Pique Technique, the

A persuasion strategy in psychology and communication studies in which a request is made in an unusual or oddly specific way, thereby “piquing” the target’s curiosity and reducing automatic refusal. For example, asking for “37 cents” instead of “some change” disrupts habitual responses and makes people more likely to comply. In the context of technology and textual studies, the pique technique illustrates how small rhetorical or design choices in phrasing, interface prompts, or notifications can increase user engagement by interrupting patterns of inattention or resistance.
Platform Capitalism

A term popularized by Nick Srnicek in his book Platform Capitalism (2017), describing a mode of economic organization in which digital platforms—such as Google, Facebook, Amazon, and Uber—extract, process, and monetize user data as their primary source of value. These platforms act as intermediaries that facilitate and control interactions between users, producers, and consumers, consolidating economic and informational power through network effects and surveillance-based data collection. Within the technological and cultural study of literacy, platform capitalism frames how digital infrastructures shape communication, creativity, and participation. It highlights the way literacy practices increasingly occur within privately owned, data-driven ecosystems that commodify human interaction. Although Melanie Walsh does not use the term explicitly in “The Challenges and Possibilities of Social Media Data” (2023), her critique of social media research methods aligns with Srnicek’s framework. She examines how scholars’ reliance on corporate APIs and restricted data pipelines situates digital humanities research within systems of platform capitalism—raising questions about ethics, access, and power in digital scholarship.
Place vs. Space

A conceptual distinction often explored in cultural geography and critical theory to differentiate between abstract, unstructured locations (space) and meaningful, socially constructed locations (place). *Space* refers to a more neutral, open, or undifferentiated area that lacks specific meaning or identity. *Place*, on the other hand, is space that has been imbued with human experiences, cultural significance, and social practices. Scholars such as Michel de Certeau and Yi-Fu Tuan emphasize that places emerge through human interactions, memories, and narratives that give spaces meaning. This distinction is crucial for understanding how locations acquire cultural, emotional, and political significance, as well as for examining the relationships between people and their environments.
Polyvocality

Refers to the inclusion and representation of multiple voices, perspectives, or points of view within a single text or discourse. In literature and cultural theory, polyvocality often involves the interplay of different characters, narrators, or ideological positions, creating a more complex, layered narrative. This technique disrupts the dominance of a single authoritative voice, promoting a multiplicity of interpretations and highlighting the diversity of experience. Polyvocality is commonly associated with postmodernism and postcolonial theory, where it is used to challenge hegemonic narratives and give voice to marginalized or suppressed perspectives.
Postmodernism

A broad intellectual and cultural movement that emerged in the mid-20th century as a reaction against modernism. It is characterized by skepticism toward grand narratives, absolute truths, and ideologies, embracing instead pluralism, fragmentation, and relativism. In literature, art, and architecture, postmodernism often features irony, pastiche, playfulness, and self-referentiality. It challenges traditional boundaries between high and low culture, and is marked by an interest in the deconstruction of language and the instability of meaning. Key thinkers associated with postmodernism include Jean-François Lyotard, Jacques Derrida, and Michel Foucault.
Procedural Enthymeme

A concept within procedural rhetoric where a game’s rules or mechanics encourage players to make inferences and fill in logical gaps based on their actions and the outcomes. It relies on the player's assumptions to complete an argument embedded in the gameplay, similar to how enthymemes work in classical rhetoric, where a premise is left unstated for the audience to infer.
Procedural Literacy

A concept developed by Ian Bogost (building on earlier work in game studies and education) to describe the ability to understand, interpret, and design processes — especially computational ones. In Persuasive Games (2007), Bogost defines procedural literacy as not only the skill of operating software or following instructions, but the deeper capacity to read and critique the rules, structures, and logics by which systems function. This literacy involves recognizing how procedures model aspects of the world, what assumptions they encode, and how alternative procedures might produce different outcomes. Within digital humanities, procedural literacy highlights the importance of teaching students and scholars to engage critically with algorithmic and rule-based media, treating processes themselves as a form of rhetoric.
Procedural Rhetoric

A concept coined by Ian Bogost in Persuasive Games: The Expressive Power of Videogames (2007), referring to the way games and other software systems make arguments and express ideas through their rules, processes, and interactions. Unlike traditional rhetoric, which relies on words or visuals, procedural rhetoric operates through the mechanics of a system, guiding players or users to understand and internalize a message by interacting with the underlying processes. This method is particularly effective in video games, where players experience ideologies and perspectives through the rules governing the game world.
Queer Cultural Analytics

A critical approach to cultural analytics outlined by Kent Chang in Debates in the Digital Humanities (2023). Queer cultural analytics interrogates the assumptions, categories, and methods of large-scale digital analysis by drawing on queer theory’s skepticism toward normativity, fixed identities, and binary logics. Rather than treating data as neutral, this approach foregrounds how queerness can unsettle dominant interpretive frameworks and open space for alternative readings, resistant patterns, and unexpected affinities.
Racial Formation Theory

A framework developed by Michael Omi and Howard Winant in Racial Formation in the United States (first edition 1986, later expanded) that explains race as a socially constructed process shaped by political, economic, and cultural forces. Rather than treating race as fixed or purely biological, racial formation theory emphasizes how racial categories are created, transformed, and contested through what the authors call racial projects — organized efforts that distribute resources, meanings, and power along racial lines. In digital humanities and media studies, the theory provides tools for examining how technologies participate in racial formation: encoding bias in algorithms, shaping online identities, or reproducing racial hierarchies through digital infrastructures.
Remediation

Remediation is the process by which new media refashion or incorporate older media. The concept explores how new media derive their significance from prior media forms, transforming and redefining the ways in which information is presented and consumed.
Rhetoric of Failure

A form of procedural rhetoric articulated by Ian Bogost in Persuasive Games (2007), where games persuade not only through success but also through the experience of failing. Unlike traditional rhetoric that depends on convincing through language or images, procedural rhetoric communicates through rules, systems, and interactions. In this frame, failure is not merely a breakdown or obstacle but a deliberate rhetorical device: by forcing players to fail, games can model the difficulty of systems, expose underlying assumptions, or highlight social and political constraints. The rhetoric of failure thus demonstrates how meaning is produced when players confront the limits of mastery, persistence, or control within a procedural system.
Rupture-talk

A concept introduced by historian Gabrielle Hecht to describe a narrative framework that emphasizes radical breaks or discontinuities in history, technology, or society. In Hecht's view, rupture-talk often oversimplifies complex historical developments by focusing on moments of technological or social revolution, overlooking the gradual and interconnected nature of change. Hecht argues that such rhetoric can obscure the continuities and persistent inequalities that underpin these so-called "ruptures." This concept is useful for critiquing historical accounts that emphasize technological determinism or portray certain events as unprecedented, ignoring deeper systemic forces or long-standing structures that evolve over time. By engaging with rupture-talk critically, scholars are encouraged to investigate how continuities shape moments of perceived rupture and how they influence the distribution of power and resources.
Semiotics

Semiotics is the study of signs and symbols, and their use or interpretation. It explores how meaning is constructed and understood through different forms of communication, including language, images, gestures, and objects.
Silence (Trouillot)

In Silencing the Past, Haitian anthropologist Michel-Rolph Trouillot discusses the concept of silence in history, referring to the deliberate or unintentional omissions of events, voices, or perspectives in historical narratives. He argues that silence occurs at multiple stages of the historical production process—such as during the creation of sources, the assembly of archives, and the interpretation of events—ultimately shaping how history is remembered or forgotten. Silence is thus a tool of power, influencing which stories are told and whose experiences are recognized.
Simulacra

A concept most closely associated with Jean Baudrillard, especially in Simulacra and Simulation (1981), referring to representations or images that no longer point back to an original reality but instead generate their own self-contained systems of meaning. Unlike simple copies, simulacra do not mask or distort the real; they replace it, creating what Baudrillard calls hyperreality — a state in which signs circulate without stable referents. In media and digital culture, simulacra describe phenomena such as virtual identities, deepfakes, or synthetic environments where the distinction between reality and representation becomes unstable or irrelevant.
Simulation Gap (Bogost)

A concept from Ian Bogost’s Persuasive Games (2007), describing the distance between a computational model (a game’s rules and processes) and the complex realities it represents. Because simulations must simplify, abstract, or omit aspects of the world, a gap inevitably emerges between the system and lived experience. Rather than being a flaw, Bogost argues this gap is rhetorically significant: it draws attention to what is included or excluded, and it invites players to reflect on the assumptions and limitations of the modeled system. The simulation gap thus becomes a core element of procedural rhetoric, showing how games persuade not just through what they simulate, but also through what they leave out.
Software Epistemology

Software epistemology examines how software shapes and is shaped by the ways we acquire, process, and validate knowledge. It explores the intersection of software design, functionality, and the epistemological assumptions embedded within code, algorithms, and interfaces. By focusing on how software structures and mediates knowledge production, access, and representation, this concept highlights the influence of digital systems on human understanding and the biases or constraints they may impose on information processing and interpretation. Software epistemology, therefore, connects technical design with broader questions about what counts as knowledge and truth in a digital age.
Studia Humanitatis and Littera Humaniore

Studia Humanitatis refers to the study of classical antiquity, including grammar, rhetoric, poetry, history, and moral philosophy, which formed the basis of a humanistic education during the Renaissance. Littera Humaniore, or "humane letters," is closely related, emphasizing the study of literature and philosophy that cultivates the intellectual and moral virtues of humanity.
Surveillance Studies

An interdisciplinary field that critically examines the practices, technologies, and cultural meanings of surveillance. As discussed by Boyles et al. in Debates in the Digital Humanities (2023), surveillance studies situates data collection and monitoring within broader systems of power, inequality, and control. It explores how surveillance operates through digital platforms, state institutions, and everyday life, shaping subjectivity and behavior. Within digital humanities, surveillance studies highlights how infrastructures of datafication intersect with questions of privacy, consent, race, class, and labor, urging scholars to interrogate not just what is visible, but who is watching, who is watched, and to what ends.
Technological Determinism

Technological determinism is the theory that technology is the principal factor driving societal change, shaping culture, values, and social structure. It suggests that technological developments determine the direction of history and human behavior, often downplaying the role of human agency and social influences.
Third Places

A concept introduced by sociologist Ray Oldenburg to describe informal, communal spaces that exist outside of home (the "first place") and work (the "second place"). Third places, such as cafes, parks, community centers, and libraries, serve as neutral grounds where people can gather, socialize, and build community. These places are characterized by inclusivity, accessibility, and a sense of belonging, providing opportunities for conversation, relaxation, and social interaction. Oldenburg emphasizes that third places play a crucial role in fostering social cohesion, democracy, and civic engagement, as they offer a space for individuals to connect and exchange ideas in an informal setting.
Transdisciplinarity

A holistic approach that transcends traditional disciplinary boundaries by involving stakeholders beyond academia, including community members, industry experts, or policymakers, to address complex societal challenges. Julie Thompson Klein emphasizes that transdisciplinarity seeks to create a shared understanding and generate knowledge that is both socially relevant and academically rigorous. It aims to produce solutions that are context-specific and applicable in real-world scenarios, blending scientific knowledge with experiential insights and values.
Trivium

In classical education, the trivium is the threefold foundation of the liberal arts: grammar, rhetoric, and logic (often called dialectic). These disciplines were considered preparatory for the more advanced quadrivium (arithmetic, geometry, music, astronomy) in medieval curricula. Grammar provided the rules of expression, rhetoric cultivated persuasive communication, and logic/dialectic trained reasoning through both formal inference and dialogical debate. The trivium established the skills of language, argument, and thought essential for scholarly and civic life. In contemporary discussions of technology and textual studies, the trivium serves as a touchstone for thinking about the evolution of literacy: how new media reshape grammar (the rules of expression), rhetoric (the art of persuasion), and logic/dialectic (the structures of reasoning and exchange) in digital contexts.
Unit Operations

A concept developed by Ian Bogost in Unit Operations: An Approach to Videogame Criticism (2006), which refers to the fundamental, discrete processes or actions that underpin systems, including video games, software, literature, and other media forms. These unit operations are modular and combinable, representing a shift from holistic, overarching systems (such as narratives) to smaller, flexible, and interactable parts. In contrast to holistic or narrative-centered readings, unit operations focus on how meaning emerges through modular, procedural interactions among parts. In digital humanities, the idea illuminates how software, games, and texts can be understood as systems of operations rather than static wholes, offering a flexible framework for thinking about computation, representation, and cultural critique.
Velvet Revolution

Originally, the term "Velvet Revolution" referred to the peaceful and non-violent transition of power that took place in Czechoslovakia in 1989, which led to the end of communist rule in the country. The term highlights the smooth and bloodless nature of the political shift. Lev Manovich adopts this metaphor to describe a similarly non-disruptive yet transformative process within digital culture and media production. In this context, the "velvet revolution" signifies the gradual integration of digital technologies into traditional media forms, resulting in an evolution rather than a complete overthrow. It characterizes how digital tools, platforms, and practices reshape cultural production in a seamless manner, blending old and new media, and fostering innovation without radical breaks. Manovich’s use of the term underscores the transformative potential of digital media to integrate and extend traditional forms rather than replace them outright, thus reflecting the hybrid and interconnected nature of contemporary culture.
Visceral Data Analysis

A method of data analysis developed by Harmony Bench and Kate Elswit that emphasizes the embodied, sensory, and affective dimensions of working with data. In their chapter “The Body is Not (Only) a Metaphor” in Debates in the Digital Humanities (2023), they argue that data work is not purely abstract or detached; it is entangled with the researcher’s bodily experience and interpretive stance. Visceral data analysis calls attention to the ways that feeling, movement, and perception shape how data is encountered, understood, and represented.
Visual Turn

A shift within the digital humanities and cultural studies that foregrounds images, visualization, and visual culture as central objects of analysis and modes of knowledge production. The visual turn responds to the increasing prominence of digital images, data visualizations, and multimodal media in contemporary culture, challenging text-centric models of scholarship. In this context, the visual is not merely illustrative but constitutive of meaning, demanding methods that engage aesthetics, representation, and power. The visual turn also emphasizes how digital tools privilege sight — through graphs, maps, dashboards, and interfaces — and calls for critical reflection on what is revealed, hidden, or distorted when knowledge is rendered visually.
