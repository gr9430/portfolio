# Reflection
Writing this policy forced me to notice where my instincts as a critical-making practitioner and my instincts as an instructor pull in different directions. The copyright question turned out easier than I expected, mostly because I built around it rather than into it: using a public-domain text for the one assignment where AI and copyrighted material could actually collide meant I never had to write rules about fair use or derivative work. That's a design choice, not a policy solution, and I want to be honest about the difference. A student working with a text still under copyright would need guidance this document doesn't give them.

The labor question was the one I almost left out entirely, which says something about how invisible that labor is even to someone who thinks about AI critically. Naming it as a resource cost rather than an abstraction — matching the scale of the tool to the scale of the task — was my attempt to make the computational and human labor behind these models legible to students without turning the policy into a lecture on data centers. Whether that's enough, or whether it undersells the point, is something I'm still unsettled about.

Equity of access mattered to me from the start, and I think the policy does real work here: the translation and Handy sections aren't accommodations bolted onto a stricter default, they're built from the premise that cognitive replacement and access are different problems that get flattened together too often. Framing AI-assisted translation as prosthesis rather than exception was a deliberate refusal of deficit thinking, not just softer language for the same rule.

Where I'm less certain is UDL, specifically representation. The policy gives students multiple means of engagement — they can defend their process orally instead of just in writing — but the document itself is dense prose with no alternate format. If I believe students should have more than one way in, that belief should extend to the policy they're reading, not just the assignments it governs.

The prohibition-versus-play question is the one I keep returning to. The policy opens with a penalty because I wanted the stakes clear before the philosophy, but I know that ordering shapes first impressions. My hope is that a student who reads past the first line finds a document more interested in their reasoning than in catching them — and that this attitude carries over into how they approach agentic tools like Claude Code, where the invitation is to build something that requires real iteration, not to find the shortest path to a finished file. Whether the policy actually produces that disposition, or just states it, is something I won't know until I see how students actually use these tools this semester.

# AI Policy
## TL;DR
- Unauthorized AI use (no credit, no documentation of process) results in an automatic 15% deduction. This isn't a gotcha—I want to understand your how and why, either in office hours or by accepting the grade.
- Do not upload a copyrighted work or a peer's work to an LLM under any circumstances.
- If English isn't your first language, or you find outlining/drafting difficult, AI-assisted translation and tools like Handy are legitimate process work, not cheating.
- We use Duck.AI in this course so you can compare models and so I can review your process via its export feature. All required tools except Handy run in-browser and are available on library computers.
- Match the scale of the tool to the scale of the task—a quick question deserves a quick answer, not an extended session.
* * *
## Unauthorized AI Use
Unauthorized AI use will result in an automatic 15% deduction from the maximum possible grade for any given assignment. Unauthorized use of AI esentially refers to anything reasonably suspected to be authored by an LLM with no proper credit given and no documentation of the process.

Documentation means the Duck.AI transcript (the .txt file DAI provides on export) along with a few representative screenshots of key interactive moments—enough for me to see the shape of the exchange, not a full transcript of every message.

This is not a "gotcha" clause, but to uncritically use AI undermines a lot of the point of this specific course. I want to understand the *how* and *why* of your AI usage because I am more interested in growth and process development than I am in outright penalty. Still, you will be expected to either prepare a discussion during an office hours appointment in a way that is not just in defense, or to accept the grade.

## Copyright Concerns
The one place AI use overlaps directly with a copyrighted text is the Textual Analysis project, and we've avoided that problem by design: our shared text is Lewis Carroll's *Alice's Adventures Under Ground*, available through Project Gutenberg and in the public domain. There's no rights conflict in the source material itself. What matters here isn't ownership of the text but whether the analysis is yours. Documentation requirements apply the same way they do everywhere else in this policy.

With that said, one should not upload a copyrighted work or a peer's work to an LLM under any circumstances.

## A Practical Case
"Practical" has different meanings based on the individual, so I want to highlight what it means to me. Let's say I have at least one book to read during my week for classes. Would it make sense for me to just have a Large Language Model (LLM) like Claude or Chat GPT read the text for me and return uninspired information? No. While it seems like it would be practical to do so amid my busy schedule, I am not be receiving authentic knowledge by reading what Claude or Chat GPT summarizes.

What I mean by authentic knowledge is the recognition of how the author's writing relates to my experiences, worldview, and values. When we read, we are investing our time into a laborious process with high reward. An LLM cannot accomplish authentic thinking for me because they are trained on massive datasets that predict statistically probable outputs that might resemble a "reasonably best effort"; in essence, think of when LLMs "drift" or "hallucinate." Think of it like this: The customer is always right; this is part of why LLMs have a reputation for being sycophantic.

### Translation and Access
When a student communicates in English as a second language, they might not have the easiest time brainstorming (see [Santos Netos 2012](https://cah.ucf.edu/writingrhetoric/wp-content/uploads/sites/27/2019/10/Stylus_4_1_Santos_Neto.pdf)). Simply put, a major barrier for anyone communicating in a language that is not their native is that they may experience ideas that are not easily translatable (if at all) between native and secondary language, or they may not immediately comprehend some of the cultural weight of certain references or idioms.

Likewise, I generally encourage students to first outline in their first language. Then, should they like, they can input that writing into an LLM like Claude to translate that writing to English (in respect to our case here, in the composition classroom). I want to be frank in saying this is not cheating; it is prosthesis. Students who use AI for translation are using a prosthetic access tool to participate in a linguistic context that was not designed with them in mind. The concern about AI in this course is cognitive replacement and not linguistic access. Failing to distinguish AI as prosthetic access from cognitive replacement is not merely a pedagogical error but a continuation of deficit thinking dressed in new technological anxiety. Even if the issue is not communicating in English as a second language, many native English communicators still have issue with things like outlining or drafting; I am one of them! A lot of folks have an easier time speaking through their ideas rather than writing them down. If this seems like the case for you, or you want to experiment with this mode of composition, then I recommend [Handy](https://handy.computer). From their website:

"I built Handy because I broke a finger, was put into a cast and as a result my hand was out of commision. I tried some of the existing speech-to-text apps but none were open source and extensible. So Handy was made to fill this gap. . . . it’s only function is to put whatever you say into a text box . . . it runs completely offline using Whisper, works across platforms, and doesn’t require subscriptions or cloud services."

So, feel free to speak through your stream of consciousness and see how you may shape your text. This is legitimate process work that many writers, including ones who do not have the same motor functions as others use. I want to bring this up, too, because a lot of students seem to defer to AI when they feel like they are up against the wall on a deadline; I think you might find it removes some of the pressure to use AI.

## The Model We Will Use
I want us all to use Duck.AI for the AI work we accomplish in this course. Duck.AI is a privacy-oriented browser and search engine DuckDuckGo's AI instance that uses many different models and acts as a mediator between you and LLMs like ChatGPT, Claude, and Gemini so that your data is not used to train their models. I want you to use Duck.AI so you may experiment with different AI models to see how they differ in application. Also, Duck.AI has a simple export feature built into its interface that allows me to evaluate how you are using AI for course work.

All required tools for this course, except Handy, run entirely in a browser, so campus library computers give you full access to everything you need.

## Using AI Strategically
Part of developing real AI literacy is learning to match the scale of the tool to the scale of the task. A single, bounded question deserves a single, bounded answer—asking Duck.AI "Summarize the plot and history of Lewis Carrol's *Alice's Adventures Underground* briefly" and reading the response once is no different from consulting an encyclopedia entry before a lecture. That is, provided you can confirm that summary against your understanding of the text.

What I want you to avoid is the mismatch: running an extended, multi-step AI session to answer what a single search would resolve, or generating five draft variations of a paragraph you could write once and revise yourself. Underneath the deference/material-shaping distinction is also this—a resource question. These models take real computational and human labor to run and train. Using more tool than a task needs isn't just a habit that weakens your thinking; it's a cost you're incurring without a reason.

## Finally
Everyone continues to evolve their understanding of AI's potential and limitations. I have been wrong about LLM functionality and use cases in the past and probably will be again. This policy is a living document but the principles undergirding it are unwavering: I want this course to help develop your skills when it comes to critical thinking and expression. Any tool that helps to serve that goal is worthy of serious consideration, and so is the ability to understand how to properly use any tool.
